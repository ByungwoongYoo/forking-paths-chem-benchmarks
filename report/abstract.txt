A benchmark score is a claim, and readers, including AI agents that summarise or extend research, usually see the number without the choices behind it. We measure how far one reported test R² can move under choices that are each individually defensible. We used three public MoleculeNet regression sets (ESOL, FreeSolv, Lipophilicity), two standard feature sets and a random forest. Across 360 train/test runs (3 split strategies × 20 split seeds × 2 feature sets × 3 datasets), random splits gave a higher mean R² than scaffold splits, by 0.050 to 0.133, and than Butina-cluster splits, by 0.035 to 0.250. Group splits, usually read as the rigorous option, were 1.2 to 3.3 times more seed-sensitive. Reporting the best of 20 split seeds raised their expected R² by 0.047 to 0.193. In 6 of 12 settings this was enough to match or beat the honest random-split mean. Reporting the best of all 120 runs per dataset overstated the cluster-split mean by 0.059 to 0.205 R². The median nearest-training-neighbour Tanimoto similarity of a split can be computed without training a model, and it tracked R² across splits (Spearman ρ 0.37 to 0.89). Reweighting random-split errors to a target split's similarity profile cut the mean absolute error of predicting group-split RMSE from 0.186 to 0.122, improving 11 of 12 settings, but it overshot on one small dataset. We propose a two-number disclosure, the split similarity profile plus the seed spread, that makes these forks visible to human and automated readers.
